Abstract

We rebuild the 32 relative-strength strategies of Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) on shares traded on India's National Stock Exchange, for the 237 holding months from January 2007 to September 2026, and report the paper's other tests where Indian data allow. Buying the top past-return decile and selling the bottom decile earns a positive return in all 64 strategy runs (32 strategies on each of two universes), about 1.5% a month for the paper's six-month ranking and six-month holding strategy against 0.95% in the original US sample, and it remains positive after Indian trading costs. Cash shares cannot be shorted overnight in India, so we also hold the winners decile alone: among the 500 most traded shares it compounded at … a year after costs against … for the NIFTY 500 total return, but it fell … from its peak in the 2008 crash, and its lead over the index came almost entirely after 2013. As in the paper, the profit is not explained by market risk and comes mainly from the winners; unlike the paper, there is no January loss and no reversal after the first year in the main sample. This page lets the reader choose any of the 64 portfolios and examine its returns, risk and place among the others.

Keywords: momentum, relative strength, market efficiency, India, NSE, replication. JEL: G11, G12, G14.

1. Introduction

Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) showed that US stocks with the highest returns over the past three to twelve months kept outperforming those with the lowest over the following three to twelve months. Their strategy ranks stocks each month on their past J-month return, forms ten equally weighted deciles, buys the top decile and sells the bottom decile, and holds each position for K months, so that K overlapping positions are held at once, each with 1/K of the capital. A second set of strategies waits one week between ranking and buying. Over 1965–1989 all 32 strategies earned positive returns, about 1% a month.

This page applies the same construction to Indian shares. The specification above chooses one of the 64 portfolios (32 strategies on each of two universes); every figure and table in Sections 2 and 3 responds to it. "The portfolio" below means the winners decile alone, equally weighted within each monthly cohort and rebalanced monthly; "the paper portfolio" means winners minus losers, which cannot be held by most investors in India. Both are measured after Indian delivery costs: securities transaction tax, stamp duty, exchange and regulatory charges and GST, plus an assumed market impact of 0.1% a side for the 500 most traded shares and 0.3% for all shares.

6. Comparison with an earlier study

An earlier study of the same paper, made with Claude Code on the same NSE files, was set beside this one only after these results were final. The 32 long-short spreads agree to within 0.02 to 0.14 percentage points a month on average, with correlations of 0.98 to 0.998 across the 16 cells of each panel, and both find the same best long-only strategy (nine-month ranking, three-month holding). The one visible difference is the effect of the skipped week: it depends on where the gap falls in the calendar month. This study ranks at month ends and starts holding five sessions later; the earlier study ends the ranking window five sessions before the month end. With the second method this study's numbers agree with theirs. The full comparison is in the repository.

7. Data, methods and caveats

Share prices come from the National Stock Exchange's daily bhavcopy files from 1995, including shares later delisted. NSE does not adjust the previous close on an ex-date, so returns are adjusted with NSE's own list of splits, bonuses, consolidations and dividends, which is reliable from 2005; daily returns below −50% or above +100% are treated as data errors and set to zero. Exchange traded funds and similar units are removed. A share is ranked in a month only if it has a return in each of the previous J months and traded in the last five sessions of the month. Universe A is every such share; universe B keeps the 500 with the highest median daily traded value over the previous 250 sessions that traded on at least 80% of them. The benchmark is the NIFTY 500 total return index. Alphas use the Indian 91-day Treasury bill return from the IIMA data library, available to December 2025.

The caveats matter. The winners-minus-losers portfolio cannot be held by most investors, and no borrowing cost is charged. Costs are 2026 rates applied to the whole sample, and market impact is assumed. Nothing is charged after a delisted share's last trade, and if every exiting winner lost 100% the six-month/six-month spread would be roughly 1.1% to 1.2% a month before costs. NSE's corporate action list is incomplete, so some bonuses are missing. Universe A includes penny stocks, mainly in the late 1990s. Size is proxied by traded value. The effect was weak in 2007–2011, and the evidence is strongest after 2011. With 64 strategies tested, a single one needs a t-statistic near 3.4 to rule out chance on a Bonferroni basis; 20 of the 64 pass on Newey–West t-statistics, although the strategies are highly correlated. The headline strategy, six-month ranking and six-month holding without a gap, was fixed in the approved plan before any result was seen. An independent reviewer reproduced the headline numbers and found no look-ahead.
